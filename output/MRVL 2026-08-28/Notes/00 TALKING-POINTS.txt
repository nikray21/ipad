• That does not make sense until you read what is actually in the filings.
• Four things buried in the 10-Q and the fine print explain what the headline beat does not.
• THEN the slide: here they are.

SLIDE 02 — A record quarter. A GAAP number that lags.   (26s)
ON SCREEN: GAAP EPS is 35% of non-GAAP. That gap is not just stock comp.

• First, the quarter as reported yesterday after the close.
• Revenue two point seven three nine billion, a record, up thirty seven percent year over year.
• Non-GAAP earnings ninety four cents a share, comfortably ahead of estimates.
• But look at the GAAP number right next to it: thirty three cents.
• Less than half.

SLIDE 03 — The biggest beat. The worst reaction.   (30s)
ON SCREEN: This was the biggest beat in six quarters. The stock fell anyway.

• Finding one.
• Marvell's stock does not trade on whether it beats guidance — it always beats guidance, by a little, like clockwork.
• Every single quarter for the last eight has landed above the midpoint the company set three months earlier.
• Today's beat, thirty nine million dollars, was the biggest of the last seven quarters.
• And the stock still fell more than ten percent.
• Look at the pattern across two years: reactions ranging from plus twenty three percent to minus twenty percent, with no relationship at all to how big the beat was.
• What actually moved it today was three lines down in the outlook: next quarter's margin guide, which came in below what the company just delivered.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

SLIDE 04 — One segment is 79% of the revenue   (28s)
ON SCREEN: Data Center: +46% year over year. Everything else: -3% quarter over quarter.

• Finding two.
• Marvell reports one blended growth number, thirty seven percent.
• That hides what's actually happening underneath.
• Data Center revenue: up forty six percent year over year, now seventy nine percent of the entire company.
• Everything else — enterprise networking, carrier infrastructure, consumer — actually shrank three percent from the quarter before.
• This is not a broad-based beat.
• It is one segment, growing very fast, carrying every other part of the business on its back.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

SLIDE 05 — A liability that grows when the stock does   (32s)
ON SCREEN: $303M of GAAP earnings, spent managing Marvell's own earnout exposure.

• Finding three, and this is the one that explains the GAAP-to-non-GAAP gap from the scoreboard slide.
• In February, Marvell paid three and a half billion dollars for a company called Celestial AI — fifty five percent of it in stock, twenty four and a half million new shares.
• Celestial's revenue so far: the filing's own words, 'not material.' But here's the part nobody flagged: part of that deal is an earnout, and its accounting value is modeled off Marvell's OWN stock price.
• As the stock ran up on AI headlines this year, that liability swelled — three hundred two million dollars of GAAP earnings drag in just two quarters, net of a hedge Marvell had to go buy specifically to manage the exposure it created.
• That's real money spent managing an accounting mechanism, not the business.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

SLIDE 06 — NVIDIA isn't just a customer here   (26s)
ON SCREEN: NVIDIA's stake is already worth 136% more than it paid.

• Finding four.
• Buried in the same note as the Celestial earnout: on March thirty first, NVIDIA bought two billion dollars of Marvell preferred stock.
• Convertible into common shares at ninety one dollars and eighty four cents each.
• Marvell trades today well over double that.
• NVIDIA is not just designing chips with Marvell — it is sitting on a stake that is already deeply in the money, and almost nobody covering this earnings report mentioned it.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

SLIDE 07 — Seven sellers. Zero real buyers.   (32s)
ON SCREEN: $48.1M sold. The only buying was a payroll-deduction plan.

• One more thing, and it took the most digging of anything in this episode: every Form 4 Marvell's executives and directors have filed in the last sixteen months, re-parsed by hand because no single filing adds this up.
• Seven people sold stock — the CEO, the COO, the Data Center president, the general counsel, and both the outgoing and incoming CFO — forty eight million dollars combined.
• Most of it, about eighty percent, was on a pre-set trading plan scheduled months ahead, which is the fair, boring explanation for most insider selling.
• But one sale wasn't on any plan: the brand new CFO, eight days into the job, sold shares with no 10b5-1 plan cited anywhere in the filing.
• And here's the part that actually surprised me: in sixteen months, the only shares anyone bought were twenty seven thousand two hundred shares, picked up by four executives on the exact same day, at the exact same price — a routine paycheck stock-purchase plan, not a single person deciding this was a good time to buy.
• Seven sellers.
• Zero real buyers.

SLIDE 08 — Cheaper than Broadcom. Growing slower too.   (22s)   [CAN CUT]
ON SCREEN: 0.9× Broadcom's multiple, but growing 11 points slower.

• So what are you paying for this?
